What you're looking at

Skywatch listens around the clock to 11175 kHz, the main frequency of the US Air Force's High Frequency Global Communications System (HFGCS), a shortwave network that reaches military aircraft anywhere in the world. Most of what you'll hear is static and routine radio checks. Every so often a station reads out an Emergency Action Message (EAM): a string of letters and digits in the phonetic alphabet, read twice.

Each box below is one character of that message as the computer heard it. A red ? is one it couldn't make out: select it to hear that moment and say what you heard. The messages are encrypted, so nobody here knows what they mean. Skywatch only writes them down.

About Skywatch

A plain-language guide to what this site listens to and how to read it.

What is HFGCS?

The High Frequency Global Communications System is the US Air Force's worldwide high-frequency (shortwave) radio network, run from Grand Forks AFB and Joint Base Andrews. Ground stations use it to reach aircraft wherever they are, including strategic bombers and airborne command posts. HF signals bounce off the upper atmosphere, so they carry across oceans, but they also fade and crackle, which is why the audio sounds rough. Its main frequency is 11175 kHz, and anyone with a shortwave receiver can listen.

What is an Emergency Action Message?

An EAM is a coded message to US strategic forces. A station calls all stations, reads a short header, then the message itself: usually about 30 letters and digits in the phonetic alphabet (“Alpha, Bravo, Seven…”). Then it says “I say again” and reads the whole thing a second time. EAMs go out regularly. They're encrypted, so the characters mean nothing to outside listeners; copying them accurately is the challenge, not decoding them.

What is a Skyking?

A shorter message that starts “Skyking, Skyking, do not answer”, followed by a codeword, a time and an authentication. Skywatch shows those three parts.

Reading the page

Listen live
Plays 11175 kHz as it is right now. Expect mostly static, with voices coming and going.
Meter and lamp
The bar shows how far the signal is above the background noise. When it passes the yellow mark, Skywatch starts recording and the lamp says “copying”.
The copy
The header, then the message one character per box, then each of the two readings underneath. Where one reading was unclear, the other fills the gap.
Red ?
A character the computer couldn't make out, or one where the two readings disagree.
Blue, underlined
A character listeners have corrected. Select any box to hear that moment and vote; once enough people agree, their answer is used.
Copy trainer
Practise copying a real recording yourself and see how you did.
Aircraft
E-6B Mercury, E-4B Nightwatch, B-52H, B-2 and WC-135 positions from public ADS-B receivers. These aircraft often fly without broadcasting a position, so an empty map is normal.

Where it comes from

The audio is public HF radio picked up by online receivers, transcribed by Whisper (opens in a new tab) speech recognition, which makes mistakes on noisy radio, hence the corrections. Aircraft positions come from adsb.lol (opens in a new tab). Background reading: Priyom (opens in a new tab), Number Stations Research (opens in a new tab) and NEET INTEL's HFGCS FAQ (opens in a new tab). Skywatch is open source on GitHub (opens in a new tab).
